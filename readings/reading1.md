# cart315
In Chapter 2 of The Stuff Games Are Made Of, “Rules: Change Comes from Within,” Pippin Barr uses Pong as an experimental baseline to test conceptual variations. By treating elements such as the paddle size, visibility, and physics as scientific variables, Barr demonstrates just how malleable a game’s fundamental constraints are. It reframes the rules as a flexible material of design rather than rigid boundaries. 

The variation that resonated most with me was Viennese Pong. While other iterations alter the game's mechanics, such as changing ball counts, paddle dimensions or visibility of different elements, Viennese Pong leaves the core system untouched. Instead, it asks a simple framing question: “Are you in Vienna?” The shift is fascinating because the game transforms entirely through a social contract rather than rewritten game physics. It relies entirely on the player’s honesty.

This reminded me of an interactive project a classmate made last semester, which asked players if they believed unicorns exist. Answering “no” locked the player into a flashing loop until they refreshed the page. If the player answered “yes,” the game revealed itself. Both designs gatekeep the experience behind a player’s response. They both rely on an honor system. The game is not able to determine whether the player is telling the truth. While lying technically grants access, it breaks the conceptual premise. A dishonest player never truly experiences the work as intended. Barr’s example made me realize that rules do not exist solely in the computer's logic. They also live in the unspoken agreement between the designer and the player.
